         Training Budget and Time Parameterisation Decide the Ranking of
             Propagation Operators on LEO Inter-Satellite-Link Graphs

                                                Phuc Hao Doa,∗
                         a
                             CAIRA, Danang Architecture University, Da Nang, Viet Nam




Abstract
A preliminary version of this study reported, from 3 seeds, that a propagation operator built from
the transition probabilities of a continuous-time quantum walk, |e−itL |2 , outperformed the heat
kernel e−tL on a 1584-satellite low-Earth-orbit inter-satellite-link graph, and read the trend across
shell sizes as a ballistic mechanism emerging with scale. This paper is a reproducibility audit of
that claim. The object under test is the probability kernel, not the unitary propagator whose
over-smoothing argument recent work has proved; the two kernels are both real, nonnegative and
row-stochastic and differ only in row profile, which is stated before any result is read. Three
operators sharing one eigenbasis and one network are compared on Walker-delta shells of 264 to
4400 satellites, on two node-field tasks, over ten seeds, across arms that vary the parameterisation
and initialisation of the learned propagation time t, the capacity and the training budget, with
two reach-matched decoupled baselines, for 1920 plus 360 training runs on one machine. With the
preliminary budget held fixed, no operator improves on a constant predictor by more than 2.9% on
any shell of 1584 satellites or more, in any arm, at any capacity; the threshold sensitivity of that
statement is reported. With a five-fold budget every shell leaves the floor, and the preliminary result
is not reproduced: under paired tests across seeds, no arm-level cell distinguishes the two spectral
kernels at the 0.05 level after Holm correction (0 of 12), the heat kernel leads in mean in 7 cells and
the walk kernel in 5, and the seed-majority verdicts the preliminary protocol would have declared
move by up to 3 seeds when the metric changes from mean error to far-node error. The ranking at
the one scale where the preliminary budget learns anything follows the t arm through a seed-level
bimodality with indistinguishable escape rates for the two kernels. Long training at a fixed learning
rate collapses 11.1% of runs to the constant solution, which is counted rather than dropped. The
released artifact of the preliminary version is shown to have used a different configuration from its
text. All code, results and the dated protocol are released.
Keywords: LEO constellations, inter-satellite links, graph neural networks, spectral propagation,
quantum walks, reproducibility, evaluation methodology



1. Introduction

   The inter-satellite-link (ISL) graph of a low-Earth-orbit (LEO) mega-constellation is large and
nearly regular, and its links across the seam and the polar regions come and go [1, 2]. Learned

connectivity decisions [5]. At each layer such a model applies a propagation operator Φ(L) to node
features and then a shared nonlinearity [6, 7], so the choice of operator is a design decision whose
consequences grow with the graph.
     Three operators span the design space studied here. The local step of a graph convolutional
network [6], equation (1) below, moves information
                                             √        one hop per layer. The heat kernel e−tL moves
it diffusively, over a range that grows like t [8]. The transition-probability kernel of a continuous-
time quantum walk [9], |e−itL |2 , is a real, row-stochastic matrix whose rows spread ballistically,
over a range that grows like t, and it can be dropped into the same layer in place of the heat
kernel [10, 11]. Recent work has made a separate and stronger argument for walk-based operators:
the unitary propagator e−itL applied to features damps no frequency and preserves feature norm,
so it resists over-smoothing [12, 13, 14]. That argument concerns the unitary operator in feature
space. It is not tested here, and we say so at the outset: the probability kernel studied in this paper
is, like the heat kernel, a real nonnegative row-stochastic matrix, and it inherits from the walk only
the shape of its rows (Section 3.2). What the probability kernel might still offer is longer reach
at equal t, and whether that translates into a better propagation operator once t is learned is an
empirical question.
     A preliminary version of this study, circulated for review in June 2026 and available in the
companion repository, answered that question in the affirmative: on a 1584-satellite shell the walk
kernel reached a mean absolute error of 0.148 against 0.168 for the heat kernel, from 3 seeds,
and the trend from smaller shells was read as the ballistic mechanism emerging with scale. This
paper is a reproducibility audit of that result. It asks whether the ranking is a property of the
two kernels or of the way they were evaluated, and it answers by making explicit and varying the
quantities the preliminary comparison left implicit: the training budget relative to graph size, the
parameterisation and initialisation of the learned propagation time t, the capacity, the number of
seeds, and the inferential rule by which a winner is declared.
     The contribution is the audit and what it finds.

   • A protocol in which the operators share one eigendecomposition, one network, one parameter
     count and one training loop; in which budget, t parameterisation, t initialisation and capacity
     are arms; in which two reach-matched decoupled propagation baselines, personalised PageR-
     ank and a power of the normalised adjacency, separate “spectral operator” from “longer reach”;
     and in which every reading rule, including the paired tests and the multiplicity correction, is
     stated with its operating characteristics before a verdict is read (Section 4).

   • Under the preliminary budget of 200 epochs, no operator improves on the constant predictor
     by more than 2.9% on any shell of 1584 satellites or more, in any arm, at either capacity
     (Section 5.1); the sensitivity of this statement to the floor threshold is tabulated. On the one
     shell where learning occurs the heat-versus-walk ranking changes with the t arm, through a
     seed-level bimodality in which the two kernels escape the constant solution at indistinguishable
     rates (Section 5.2).

2. Related work

Propagation operators and their reach. Spectral graph convolutions began as polynomial filters of
the Laplacian [15, 7]; the graph convolutional network of Kipf and Welling [6] keeps the first-
order term and applies a normalised adjacency once per layer, so depth bounds reach. Stacking
layers drives features toward a constant, the over-smoothing effect identified by Li et al. [16],
shown by Oono and Suzuki [17] to be exponential in depth, and surveyed by Rusch et al. [18];
remedies include decoupling propagation from transformation as in SGC [19] and personalised-
PageRank propagation [20], and residual and identity terms as in GCNII [21]. Alon and Yahav [22]
describe the complementary bottleneck by which information from exponentially many distant
nodes is squashed into fixed-size vectors, which Topping et al. [23] relate to negative curvature
of the graph. Continuous-time views replace the discrete step with a differential equation on the
graph: GRAND [8] uses diffusion, whose solution is the heat kernel; PDE-GCN [24] and graph-
coupled oscillator networks [25] use wave-type dynamics, whose reach grows linearly in time rather
than as a square root. The distinction between diffusive and ballistic spreading that motivates the
walk kernel in this paper is the same distinction, and the wave-type models are its classical form.

Quantum walks as graph operators. The continuous-time quantum walk of Farhi and Gutmann [9]
evolves an amplitude vector by e−itL and spreads ballistically; Kempe [26] and Mülken and Blu-
men [27] review the walk and its transport properties on complex networks, and Childs [28] shows
it is computationally universal. Dernbach et al. [10] and Verdon et al. [11] were among the first to
place walk-derived operators inside trainable graph models. GraphQWalk [14] builds unsupervised,
position-independent structural embeddings from walk transition probabilities. CTQWformer [13]
learns the walk Hamiltonian and feeds final-time probabilities into a graph transformer. CTQW-
GNN [12], a preprint at the time of writing, uses the unitary propagator itself in feature space
and proves that, because its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, no frequency is damped and the
Dirichlet energy of the features cannot decay exponentially with depth. That result concerns the
unitary operator acting on features. The operator studied here is the transition-probability kernel
|e−itL |2 , a real nonnegative matrix that shares the ballistic profile of the walk but not the norm-
preserving property; the distinction matters for interpreting the results (Section 6). Quantum walks
on communication networks have also been used directly, for instance the Hadamard-walk model
for network identification of Liang et al. [29].

Graph learning on LEO constellations. Handley [30] first argued that inter-satellite routing in a
mega-constellation is a latency question, Bhattacherjee and Singla [1] fix the +Grid inter-satellite
topology that most later work assumes, Kassing et al. [31] provide the Hypatia simulator for such

of reach, which is the gap this study addresses. The tasks used here are deliberately decoupled from
any particular routing or traffic-engineering system, so that the propagation question is isolated

Evaluation pitfalls. Shchur et al. [32] showed that the ranking of graph neural network architectures
on standard benchmarks changes with the data split and the seed, Henderson et al. [33] made
the corresponding point for deep reinforcement learning, where few seeds and unreported tuning
produce rankings that do not replicate, Bouthillier et al. [34] quantify how much benchmark variance
comes from sources other than the method, and Pineau et al. [35] report on the reproducibility
programme that turned such findings into checklists. The present study applies the same discipline
to a comparison of propagation operators, and adds two quantities specific to spectral operators,
the training budget relative to graph size and the parameterisation of the learned propagation time.

3. Setting
3.1. Constellation graphs
    Each graph is a snapshot of a Walker-delta shell. Satellite positions follow circular-orbit prop-
agation in an Earth-centred inertial frame; inter-satellite links follow the +Grid pattern [1], two
intra-plane and two inter-plane links per satellite, with inter-plane links suppressed above 70◦
latitude and across the counter-rotating seam. The graph is restricted to its largest connected com-
ponent. Four shells are used (Table 1). The 264-satellite shell is the main scale of the preliminary
version; the 1584-satellite shell reproduces the geometry of the first Starlink shell [2], 72 planes of
22 satellites at 53◦ and 550 km; the 3168- and 4400-satellite shells are scale-ups of that geometry,
72 planes of 44 and 100 planes of 44, and are synthetic in the sense that no operator flies them.
They exist to make the diameter grow.
    Each training or evaluation instance draws a snapshot time uniformly over the orbital period
and a destination satellite uniformly over the component. Instance construction is seeded, so every
instance is reproducible from its index.

the study can and cannot say. Both (2) and (3) are real, nonnegative and row-stochastic; applied
to a feature matrix, both average neighbouring features with nonnegative weights and both damp
high-frequency components. The unitary propagator e−itL of CTQW-GNN [12] is neither of these
things, and its over-smoothing argument does not transfer to (3) and is not tested here. The two
kernels differ in the profile of the row as a function of graph distance, monotone for the heat kernel
and oscillatory for the walk kernel, and in how that profile’s width grows with t; given that the
network trains t, it is plausible from the definitions alone that the two will be hard to tell apart,
and the experiments below are the test of that expectation, not of the unitary argument.
    Figure 1 makes the difference concrete on a path graph, where distance from the source is a
single coordinate. A row of the heat kernel is a bell whose spatial variance grows as t1.00 ; a row of
the walk kernel is a pair of fronts whose variance grows as t2.00 , with oscillations between them. The
exponents are fitted from the kernels themselves. This is the ballistic-versus-diffusive distinction in
its cleanest form: at equal t the walk reaches farther, and its reach grows faster with t. Whether
that translates into a better propagation operator once t is learned is the empirical question of this
paper.

4. Protocol

4.1. Parameter matching and the arms
     The operators share depth, width, weight shapes and training loop, so a difference in error is
attributable to the operator and not to capacity. Two capacities are used: width 16 with three
layers, the default of the released code of the preliminary version (612 parameters including the
tℓ ), and width 32 with four layers, which the text of the preliminary version describes and its
released results were produced with (3301 parameters). Adam [36] at learning rate 0.01, eight
training and six evaluation instances per seed, and full-batch updates are used throughout. The
six evaluation instances of a seed are drawn with that seed’s own generator, so the ten seeds of a
cell evaluate on sixty distinct snapshot-destination pairs; seed-to-seed dispersion therefore includes
instance-to-instance variation.
     Four quantities are arms rather than fixed choices.

   • Parameterisation of t. A pilot run under the released parameterisation showed the learned
     tℓ going negative in the second and third layers, where relu(tℓ ) = 0 turns the propagator into
     the identity and stops its gradient, so that a model reported as three or four layers of spectral
     propagation was propagating in one. The softplus parameterisation removes this; the relu
     parameterisation is kept as a control so that the preliminary configuration is one of the arms.

   • Initialisation of t. t0 ∈ {0.5, 2}, since the same pilot showed the learned reach, and with it
     the operator ranking, depending on where t starts.

   • Capacity. The two configurations above.

   • Budget. 200 epochs, the preliminary value, held fixed across scales; and 1000 epochs for the
     second grid.

4.2. Reading rules, declared before the runs, with their operating characteristics
    Six rules govern how a cell, one (task, shell, arm) combination, is read. The first five were fixed
before the first grid was launched, and two of them were tightened after the first runs exposed a
gap; both changes are dated in the repository and stated here. The sixth was added after external
review of a draft showed that the fifth, as stated, had a false-positive rate the draft never computed.
  1. Constant baseline. Every cell reports the MAE of the predictor that outputs the mean of
     the target. Whether an operator beats it is a result, never a filter.
  2. Pathology. A run is excluded only for a training pathology: a non-finite loss, or a loss that
     rises by more than 1% between successive thirds of training. Excluded runs are counted next
     to every number they affect, and Section 5.6 reports whether each excluded run had left the
     constant solution before collapsing, since exclusion after collapse removes non-escaping runs
     preferentially. A run whose loss is flat from the start because it sits on the constant solution
     is a plateau, a legitimate outcome, and is kept. The first draft of this rule required a 10%
     loss drop and would have discarded plateaus as failures; it was corrected when the first grid
     produced them.
  3. Floor. Let ēop be the mean MAE of an operator over its valid seeds in a cell and ē0 that of
     the constant predictor. The cell’s gain is

5.7. Cost, and the scale at which the method is feasible
    Table 16 reports the median wall-clock time per run on one consumer GPU with 24 GB of
memory, with the eigendecomposition of each instance cached. The spectral operators build an
N × N propagator per layer per instance per epoch from the eigenbasis, an O(N 3 ) product that
dominates at scale; the walk kernel builds two such products, for the real and imaginary parts, and
squares them. On the 4400-satellite shell at 200 epochs a walk run takes 169 s against 81 s for the
heat kernel and 21 s for the GCN, whose propagator is sparse. On CPU, before caching, the same
cell took 1096 s, which is why the preliminary version stopped at 1584 satellites. As implemented,
with dense N × N single-precision matrices for the eigenbasis, the propagator and their gradients,

6. Discussion

What was tested, and what was not. The object of this audit is the transition-probability kernel (3).
It is a real nonnegative row-stochastic matrix, exactly as the heat kernel is, and applying it to
features damps and mixes them as a diffusion step does; what it inherits from the walk is the shape
of its rows, an oscillatory profile whose width grows linearly with t rather than as a square root
(Figure 1). Once t is a trained parameter, the heat kernel reaches as far by learning a larger t,
which the right panel of Figure 5 shows it does, in the first layer and in proportion to the shell.
That the two kernels are then indistinguishable is what the operator definitions make plausible,
and it is what the paired tests find. The argument of CTQW-GNN [12] for the unitary propagator
in feature space, which damps no frequency and preserves feature norm, is a different object and is
neither confirmed nor refuted here.

What the floor means for learned propagation on constellations. A budget that suffices on a 264-
satellite shell does not on a 1584-satellite one, and the failure is silent: the run reports a finite,
decreasing loss and an error that looks like a number. Comparisons of learned propagation across
constellation sizes that hold the budget fixed will rank noise unless they carry the constant baseline
beside every result.

What the seed-level bimodality and the vote noise mean. The split in Figure 4, with escape rates
of 57% and 58% (p = 0.95), means that at the preliminary budget a majority verdict counts which
seeds happened to escape. At the larger budget the majority statistic still moves by up to 3 seeds
between two correlated metrics on the same runs (Section 5.4), which is the margin by which most
of its verdicts were declared. The preliminary version’s 3-seed result at 1584 satellites sits inside
the range that this bimodality produces; its direction is not confirmed at ten seeds and, under
inference, no direction is.

Consequences for reporting. These are not new rules, and they are not the contribution; they
are what this audit needed to avoid repeating its own preliminary version. Carry the constant
predictor beside every result. Let the budget grow with the graph, or show that it need not. Treat
any learned reach parameter’s parameterisation and initialisation as an arm. Use a paired test with
                                                      18
a multiplicity correction, and state the false-positive rate of any vote rule that is reported beside
it. Count collapsed runs and report whether they had escaped.

What would change the verdict. A propagator applied in feature space, unitary rather than stochas-
tic, would test the argument of CTQW-GNN [12] directly; the wave-type models PDE-GCN [24]
and graph-coupled oscillator networks [25] are the classical form of the same argument and were not
run. A learning-rate schedule would reduce the collapse rate of Section 5.6 and might decide cells
that are now undecided. A task with load, in which the field to be predicted depends on traffic and
not only on geometry, and a time-varying evaluation that exercises the link churn the introduction
invokes, would say whether any operator ranking survives contact with a network function. None
of these is a small addition, and the present paper claims nothing about them.

7. Threats to validity

    The two largest shells are synthetic, and all graphs are static. The 3168- and 4400-
satellite graphs scale the Starlink shell-1 geometry and are not deployed constellations; they serve
to grow the diameter. Every instance is an independent snapshot, so the time-varying property
that motivates learned propagation on these graphs is never exercised.
    The tasks are node fields, not network functions. Hop and delay fields isolate propagation
from load, which is what the comparison needs, at the cost of distance from any routing or traffic-
engineering objective. The findings bear on the evaluation of propagation operators, not on the
routing or resource-management systems that might use them.
    The floor threshold was chosen with the data in view. Rule 3’s value was set after the
4400-satellite cells were seen, as Section 4.2 states; Table 14 reports every dependent count at four
values of it.
    The majority rule has a 34.4% null false-positive rate. It is kept only so that the
preliminary verdicts are reproducible, and no conclusion rests on it; the inference of rule 6 is what
the paper stands on.
    Exclusion is differential. Rule 2 removes runs after they have collapsed to the constant
solution, so it removes non-escapers preferentially, and the GCN loses more runs than the spectral
kernels (Table 15). Means are therefore optimistic, most for the GCN.
    One learning rate, one optimiser, six evaluation instances per seed. The collapse rate
is specific to Adam at 0.01 for 1000 epochs. Each seed evaluates on its own six instances, so the
sixty instances of a cell are distinct, but instance-level dispersion within a seed is not reported.
    The eigenbasis is cached in single precision. Training already casts the eigenpairs to single
precision, so caching them is numerically identical to recomputing, and the cached and recomputed
runs of a test cell agreed to 16 significant digits; but every arm shares one eigendecomposition per
instance, and a numerical error in it would affect all arms alike.
    Several references are preprints. CTQW-GNN [12], CTQWformer [13] and the LEO graph-
learning work of Gu et al. [4] are unrefereed at the time of writing, and GraphQWalk [14] is a recent
journal article. The argument this audit declines to test rests on the first of these; the audit itself
rests on the preliminary version, which is not in the refereed record either.
    The preliminary artifact does not reproduce its own headline. The released results of
the preliminary version record 3301 parameters for the 1584-satellite experiment, which corresponds
to the width-32, four-layer network its text describes, while the released code defaults to width 16
and three layers (612 parameters) and contains no script for that experiment. The preliminary
                                                  19
version also ran without the seam cut that its text states. The present study fixes the seam cut on,
runs both capacities, and releases the script for every number.

8. Conclusion

    We audited a preliminary result of our own: that the transition-probability kernel of a continuous-
time quantum walk outperformed the heat kernel on a 1584-satellite ISL graph and did so increas-
ingly with scale. The result does not reproduce. Under the preliminary budget the large shells are
a floor on which no operator learns; at the one shell where learning occurs the ranking follows the
parameterisation and initialisation of the learned propagation time through a seed-level bimodality
with indistinguishable escape rates; and with a budget that clears the floor, paired tests across ten
seeds separate the two kernels in no arm-level cell after multiplicity correction, on either metric.
The two kernels are the same kind of object, a real row-stochastic propagator with a learned reach,
and they behave like it. What the audit adds beyond the non-reproduction is measured: a silent
budget floor that scales with the graph, a propagation-time parameterisation that decides rankings
at small budgets, a seed-vote statistic whose noise amplitude is two to three seeds on these data,
and a collapse rate of a tenth of long runs. Each is a reason to distrust a small-seed operator
comparison on constellation graphs, including the one we published first.
