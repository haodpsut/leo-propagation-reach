         Training Budget and Time Parameterisation Decide the Ranking of
             Propagation Operators on LEO Inter-Satellite-Link Graphs

Abstract
A preliminary version of this study reported, from 3 seeds, that the transition-probability kernel of a
continuous-time quantum walk, |e−itL |2 , outperforms the heat kernel e−tL as a propagation operator
on a 1584-satellite low-Earth-orbit inter-satellite-link graph. This paper is a reproducibility audit
of that claim. Three operators sharing one eigenbasis and one network are compared on Walker-
delta shells of 264 to 4400 satellites, two node-field tasks and ten seeds, across arms that vary the
parameterisation and initialisation of the learned propagation time t, the capacity, the budget and
the link pattern of the graph, against fixed-reach baselines whose reach is measured, in 2920 plus
1350 training runs. Under the preliminary budget no operator improves on a constant predictor by
more than 2.9% on any shell of 1584 satellites or more. With a five-fold budget every shell leaves
the floor; on the torus the heat kernel is better on the smallest shell on both tasks (ten seeds of ten,
Holm-corrected p = 0.012) and the paired tests certify no direction on the larger shells, where their
minimum detectable effect exceeds the observed differences. Against the best tuned fixed-reach
baseline a learned t is ahead by 16.4% at 1584 satellites in its best arm and behind in its worst. On
the seam-cut graph, which both versions described and neither had run, the preliminary direction
at 1584 satellites is recovered at the corrected level. Which long runs collapse changes with the
floating-point precision. All code, results and the dated protocol are released.
Keywords: LEO constellations, inter-satellite links, graph neural networks, spectral propagation,
quantum walks, reproducibility, evaluation methodology



1. Introduction

    The inter-satellite-link (ISL) graph of a low-Earth-orbit (LEO) mega-constellation is large and
nearly regular, and its links across the seam and the polar regions come and go [1, 2]. Learned
models that propagate information over this graph have been proposed for routing [3, 4] and for
connectivity decisions [5]. At each layer such a model applies a propagation operator Φ(L) to node
features and then a shared nonlinearity [6, 7], so the choice of operator is a design decision whose
consequences grow with the graph.
    Three operators span the design space studied here. The local step of a graph convolutional
network [6], equation (1) below, moves information one hop per layer. The heat kernel e−tL moves

time quantum walk [9], |e−itL |2 , is a real, row-stochastic matrix whose rows spread ballistically,
over a range that grows like t, and it can be dropped into the same layer in place of the heat
kernel [10, 11]. Recent work has made a separate and stronger argument for walk-based operators:
the unitary propagator e−itL applied to features damps no frequency and preserves feature norm,
so it resists over-smoothing [12, 13, 14]. That argument concerns the unitary operator in feature
space. It is not tested here, and we say so at the outset: the probability kernel studied in this paper
is, like the heat kernel, a real nonnegative row-stochastic matrix, and it inherits from the walk only
the shape of its rows (Section 3.2). What the probability kernel might still offer is longer reach
at equal t, and whether that translates into a better propagation operator once t is learned is an
empirical question.
     A preliminary version of this study, circulated for review in June 2026 and available in the
companion repository, answered that question in the affirmative: on a 1584-satellite shell the walk
kernel reached a mean absolute error of 0.148 against 0.168 for the heat kernel, from 3 seeds,
and the trend from smaller shells was read as the ballistic mechanism emerging with scale. This
paper is a reproducibility audit of that result. It asks whether the ranking is a property of the
two kernels or of the way they were evaluated, and it answers by making explicit and varying the
quantities the preliminary comparison left implicit: the training budget relative to graph size, the
parameterisation and initialisation of the learned propagation time t, the capacity, the number of
seeds, and the inferential rule by which a winner is declared.
     The contribution is the audit and what it finds, and what it adds to the correction of one
unpublished report is a method for reading such comparisons that is itself measured: the false-
positive rate of the vote rule the preliminary protocol used, the minimum effect the paired tests
can detect, the reach of every operator on the graph, and the sensitivity of a single run to the
arithmetic.

   • A protocol in which the operators share one eigendecomposition, one network, one parameter
     count and one training loop; in which budget, t parameterisation, t initialisation, capacity
     and the link pattern of the graph are arms; in which fixed-reach decoupled propagation

2. Related work
Propagation operators and their reach. Spectral graph convolutions began as polynomial filters of
the Laplacian [15, 7]; the graph convolutional network of Kipf and Welling [6] keeps the first-
order term and applies a normalised adjacency once per layer, so depth bounds reach. Stacking
layers drives features toward a constant, the over-smoothing effect identified by Li et al. [16],
shown by Oono and Suzuki [17] to be exponential in depth, and surveyed by Rusch et al. [18];
remedies include decoupling propagation from transformation as in SGC [19] and personalised-
PageRank propagation [20], and residual and identity terms as in GCNII [21]. Alon and Yahav [22]
describe the complementary bottleneck by which information from exponentially many distant
nodes is squashed into fixed-size vectors, which Topping et al. [23] relate to negative curvature
of the graph. Continuous-time views replace the discrete step with a differential equation on the
graph: GRAND [8] uses diffusion, whose solution is the heat kernel; PDE-GCN [24] and graph-
coupled oscillator networks [25] use wave-type dynamics, whose reach grows linearly in time rather
than as a square root. The distinction between diffusive and ballistic spreading that motivates the
walk kernel in this paper is the same distinction, and the wave-type models are its classical form.

Quantum walks as graph operators. The continuous-time quantum walk of Farhi and Gutmann [9]
evolves an amplitude vector by e−itL and spreads ballistically; Kempe [26] and Mülken and Blu-
men [27] review the walk and its transport properties on complex networks, and Childs [28] shows
it is computationally universal. Dernbach et al. [10] and Verdon et al. [11] were among the first to
place walk-derived operators inside trainable graph models. GraphQWalk [14] builds unsupervised,
position-independent structural embeddings from walk transition probabilities. CTQWformer [13]
learns the walk Hamiltonian and feeds final-time probabilities into a graph transformer. CTQW-
GNN [12], a preprint at the time of writing, uses the unitary propagator itself in feature space
                                                  3
and proves that, because its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, no frequency is damped and the
Dirichlet energy of the features cannot decay exponentially with depth. That result concerns the
unitary operator acting on features. The operator studied here is the transition-probability kernel
|e−itL |2 , a real nonnegative matrix that shares the ballistic profile of the walk but not the norm-
preserving property; the distinction matters for interpreting the results (Section 6). Quantum walks
on communication networks have also been used directly, for instance the Hadamard-walk model
for network identification of Liang et al. [29].

Graph learning on LEO constellations. Handley [30] first argued that inter-satellite routing in a
mega-constellation is a latency question, Bhattacherjee and Singla [1] fix the +Grid inter-satellite
topology that most later work assumes, Kassing et al. [31] provide the Hypatia simulator for such
networks, and del Portillo et al. [2] compare the announced constellations, including the Starlink
shell whose geometry the 1584-satellite graph here reproduces. Learned models on this graph
include the link-identified routing architecture of Zhang et al. [3], the duality-guided graph learning
for joint connectivity and routing of Gu et al. [4], and machine-learning-driven cellular-satellite
multi-connectivity [5]. None of these compares propagation operators under one training protocol
with the reach of every operator, learned or fixed, measured on the graph rather than asserted,
which is the gap this study addresses. The tasks used here are deliberately decoupled from any
particular routing or traffic-engineering system, so that the propagation question is isolated from
questions of load.

Evaluation pitfalls. Shchur et al. [32] showed that the ranking of graph neural network architectures
on standard benchmarks changes with the data split and the seed, Henderson et al. [33] made
the corresponding point for deep reinforcement learning, where few seeds and unreported tuning
produce rankings that do not replicate, Bouthillier et al. [34] quantify how much benchmark variance
comes from sources other than the method, and Pineau et al. [35] report on the reproducibility
programme that turned such findings into checklists. The present study applies the same discipline
to a comparison of propagation operators, and adds two quantities specific to spectral operators,
the training budget relative to graph size and the parameterisation of the learned propagation time.

3. Setting

3.1. Constellation graphs
    Each graph is a snapshot of a Walker-delta shell. Satellite positions follow circular-orbit prop-
agation in an Earth-centred inertial frame; inter-satellite links follow the +Grid pattern [1], two
intra-plane and two inter-plane links per satellite. The generator suppresses inter-plane links above
70◦ latitude and can remove them across the counter-rotating seam. In every run of the two main
grids the seam links are kept, and at 53◦ inclination no satellite reaches 70◦ , so every snapshot of the
main grids is the full 4-regular torus of P × S satellites and 2N links; snapshots differ only in their
link lengths. The preliminary version and the first drafts of this paper stated that the seam was cut;
it was not, in either, and Section 7 records how the generator’s flag was misread. A seam-cut arm,
in which the inter-plane links between the last and the first plane are removed so that 2S satellites
drop to degree three and the graph is no longer vertex-transitive, is run at the larger budget and
reported in Section 5.5. The graph is restricted to its largest connected component. Four shells are
used (Table 1). The 264-satellite shell is the main scale of the preliminary version; the 1584-satellite
shell reproduces the geometry of the first Starlink shell [2], 72 planes of 22 satellites at 53◦ and
                                                   4
550 km; the 3168- and 4400-satellite shells are scale-ups of that geometry, 72 planes of 44 and 100
planes of 44, and are synthetic in the sense that no operator flies them. They exist to make the
diameter grow.

3.3. Network, parameterisation of t, and tasks

     The network has K layers, each computing H(ℓ+1) = σ Φ(ℓ) H(ℓ) W(ℓ) with a ReLU nonlin-

that the two parameterisations start at the same value. The GCN operator has no t.

degree. Two node-field targets are used. With d the destination, h(i, d) the geodesic hop count and

measure. A torus is vertex-transitive, so the hop field from any destination is the same field up to
a relabelling of the nodes, and the network, whose only inputs are the destination indicator and
the (constant) normalised degree, is equivariant to that relabelling. On the hop field the evaluation
instances of the main grids are therefore copies of the training instances, and the training error
equals the test error exactly (Table 13); the hop-field task on the torus is a fitting problem on one

snapshot, and differs between instances. On the seam-cut graph the destination’s position relative
to the seam changes the hop field, and the instances differ on both tasks (Section 5.5).

4. Protocol

4.1. Parameter matching and the arms
     The operators share depth, width, weight shapes and training loop, so a difference in error is
attributable to the operator and not to capacity. Two capacities are used: width 16 with three
layers, the default of the released code of the preliminary version (612 parameters including the
tℓ ), and width 32 with four layers, which the text of the preliminary version describes and its
released results were produced with (3301 parameters). Adam [36] at learning rate 0.01, eight
training and six evaluation instances per seed, and full-batch updates are used throughout. The
six evaluation instances of a seed are drawn with that seed’s own generator, so the ten seeds of a
cell evaluate on sixty distinct snapshot-destination pairs; seed-to-seed dispersion therefore includes
instance-to-instance variation.
     Four quantities are arms rather than fixed choices.

   • Parameterisation of t. A pilot run under the released parameterisation showed the learned
     tℓ going negative in the second and third layers, where relu(tℓ ) = 0 turns the propagator into
     the identity and stops its gradient, so that a model reported as three or four layers of spectral
     propagation was propagating in one. The softplus parameterisation removes this; the relu
     parameterisation is kept as a control so that the preliminary configuration is one of the arms.

   • Initialisation of t. t0 ∈ {0.5, 2}, since the same pilot showed the learned reach, and with it
     the operator ranking, depending on where t starts.

   • Capacity. The two configurations above.

   • Budget. 200 epochs, the preliminary value, held fixed across scales; and 1000 epochs for the
     second grid.

   • Graph. The torus of the main grids, and the seam-cut graph, run at 1000 epochs in all four
     t arms (Section 5.5).

Grid 1 runs both capacities, all four t arms, four shells, two tasks, three operators and ten seeds at
200 epochs (1920 runs). Grid 2 runs the width-32 configuration at 1000 epochs on the three smaller
shells with 4 t arms (720 runs). With the fixed-reach baselines below (160 runs at 200 epochs and
120 at 1000), the two main grids comprise 2920 training runs; the baseline ladder, the seam-cut
arm and the precision check of Section 5.7 add 480, 840 and 30. All runs were executed on one
machine, one GPU per arm, with the eigendecomposition of each instance cached in single precision

not settled by that identity; it is tested directly by rerunning one cell entirely in double precision

4.2. Reading rules, declared before the runs, with their operating characteristics
    Six rules govern how a cell, one (task, shell, arm) combination, is read. The first five were fixed
before the first grid was launched, and two of them were tightened after the first runs exposed a gap;
both changes are dated in the repository and stated here. The sixth was added after external review
of a draft showed that the fourth, as stated, had a false-positive rate the draft never computed, and
its unit of inference was changed after a second review showed that the first form of the rule had,
by construction, almost no power; both forms are reported.
  1. Constant baseline. Every cell reports, on every metric it is scored on, the error of the
     predictor that outputs the mean of the target, in the tables themselves. Whether an operator
     beats it is a result, never a filter.
  2. Pathology. A run is excluded only for a training pathology: a non-finite loss, or a loss that
     rises by more than 1% between successive thirds of training. Excluded runs are counted next
     to every number they affect, and Section 5.7 reports whether each excluded run had left the
     constant solution before collapsing, since exclusion after collapse removes non-escaping runs
     preferentially. A run whose loss is flat from the start because it sits on the constant solution
     is a plateau, a legitimate outcome, and is kept. The first draft of this rule required a 10%
     loss drop and would have discarded plateaus as failures; it was corrected when the first grid
     produced them.

     majority are 0.344, 0.109 and 0.039. A majority verdict is therefore reported beside, never
     instead of, the tests of rule 6, and every table that prints a verdict also prints which operator
     led in mean, so that a “tie” cannot hide a lead.
  5. Robustness. A ranking at a (task, shell) is claimed only if it is the same in every t arm run
     at that budget. Grid 2 runs 4 arms, and the number is stated wherever the rule is applied.
  6. Inference. The unit of inference is the (task, shell) pair, of which there are six at the larger
     budget. For each seed s, the per-seed difference ds = eheat,s − ewalk,s is averaged over the t
     arms in which seed s is valid for both kernels, giving one paired difference per seed; the ten
     values are summarised by their mean with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval, by the exact
     two-sided sign test and by the exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and the Wilcoxon
     p-values are Holm-corrected over the six pairs of the same metric. A difference between the
     two kernels is claimed at a (task, shell) only where the corrected p is below 0.05. The same
     statistics are also reported for every arm-level cell, with Holm correction over the 24 cells, as
     the first draft did; these per-cell tests are descriptive, for the reason given next. The tests were
     checked against an independent implementation on random data and agree to nine decimal
     places.

Operating characteristics of rule 6. The exact Wilcoxon test on n non-zero pairs cannot return a
two-sided p below 2/2n , and Holm over m tests requires the smallest p to fall below 0.05/m. Table 3
applies this to the arm-level cells of grid 2: with m = 24 the threshold is 0.00208, which n = 9 pairs
cannot reach (2/29 = 0.00391) and n = 10 pairs reach only on a unanimous 10:0 split (0.00195). Of
the 24 cells, 18 have nine or fewer valid pairs after exclusions and cannot reject at any effect size,
and the remaining 6 require every seed to agree. A “0 of 24” under that rule is close to a property
of the design, which is why the first draft’s headline, which reported exactly that, was withdrawn.
The table also gives, for each |S|, the minimum detectable effect (MDE) at the uncorrected 0.05
level and 80% power, computed by simulation from each cell’s own seed dispersion. Pooling over
arms gives every (task, shell) pair its full ten seeds and reduces m to 6, so the threshold becomes
0.0083 and a 10:0 or a favourable 9:1 split can reject. The MDE of the pooled test at the corrected
level is reported beside every pooled result (Table 12); it is what the design can resolve, and where
the observed difference is smaller than it the correct statement is that the design cannot certify a
difference, not that none exists. Dependence among the tests is stated rather than assumed away:
the four arms at a (task, shell) share instances, seeds and eigenbases, which is why they are pooled
into one test, and the two tasks of a shell share graphs and seeds, so the six pooled tests are not
independent. Holm’s procedure controls the family-wise error rate under arbitrary dependence, so
the correction remains valid; it is conservative, not invalid, under this structure.
    All six rules are implemented once, in the analysis script that produces every table and figure,
and the same script prints the pathology and plateau counts beside every mean. Figure 2 summarises
the design, and Table 4 collects the headline numbers with the section that derives each.

5. Results

5.1. A fixed budget produces a floor at scale
    Figure 3 and Table 5 show, for each capacity and each t arm, the gain (6) of the best operator
over the constant predictor as the shell grows, at the preliminary budget of 200 epochs. On the
264-satellite shell the gain on the hop field is between 7.7% and 28.7% at the released capacity
and between 24.9% and 40.3% at the submitted capacity; on the delay field it is between 7.9%
and 19.5% and between 10.9% and 29.2%. On every larger shell it never exceeds 2.9%, in any of
the eight capacity-by-arm combinations, on either task. Of the 24 hop-field cells at 1584 satellites
and above, 0 are rankable at the declared 5% floor; Section 5.6 shows that 2 become rankable at a
2% floor and 5 at 1%, all at 1584 satellites, and Table 6 lists the 84 of 1436 valid large-shell runs
that end more than 2% below the constant predictor: they concentrate at 1584 satellites, the walk
kernel contributes 34 of them and the heat kernel 29, and the largest single-run gain among them
is 18.3%. Of 1920 runs, 5 were excluded as pathologies and 48 were plateaus in the sense of rule 2,
kept.
    Doubling the width and adding a layer does not change this: the submitted capacity is as far
below the floor at 1584 satellites as the released one. Nor does the t arm. The floor is therefore not
a capacity effect and not a parameterisation effect; Section 5.3 shows that it is a budget effect.

5.7. Long training collapses a tenth of the runs
    Of the 720 runs of grid 2, 87 (12.1%) were excluded under rule 2. Inspection shows one pattern:
the loss falls, then rises again by up to 88%, and the run ends on the constant solution. Table 21
breaks the count down by operator and records whether each excluded run had escaped the constant
solution before collapsing: the GCN collapses in 36/240 runs (12 of them escapers), the heat kernel
in 24/240 (7) and the walk kernel in 27/240 (10); the fixed-reach baselines of (5) collapse in 8/60
(PPR) and 7/60 (SGC) runs. The instability is not specific to the spectral operators or to the
learned t, and because most excluded runs had not escaped, exclusion removes non-escaping runs
preferentially and every surviving mean is biased toward the escapers; the GCN, with the highest
exclusion rate, is biased most. The paired tests of Section 5.3 are computed on the seeds valid for
both kernels, so a seed removed for one kernel is removed for both. A fixed learning rate that is
adequate for 200 epochs is not stable for 1000 on these graphs; we report the rate rather than tune
it away.

5.8. Cost, and the scale at which the method is feasible
    Table 23 reports the median wall-clock time per run on one consumer GPU with 24 GB of
memory, with the eigendecomposition of each instance cached. The spectral operators build an
N × N propagator per layer per instance per epoch from the eigenbasis, an O(N 3 ) product that
dominates at scale; the walk kernel builds two such products, for the real and imaginary parts, and
squares them. On the 4400-satellite shell at 200 epochs a walk run takes 169 s against 81 s for the
heat kernel and 21 s for the GCN, whose propagator is sparse. On CPU, before caching, the same
cell took 1096 s, which is why the preliminary version stopped at 1584 satellites. As implemented,
with dense N × N single-precision matrices for the eigenbasis, the propagator and their gradients,
                                                         25
the method fits in 24 GB up to roughly N = 19300 nodes; beyond that it needs a factorised or
sparse propagator, which is a different implementation. The mega-constellations of the introduction
lie at the edge of that range and beyond it. For a comparison across scales, the cost of the operator
is part of what is being compared.

6. Discussion

What was tested, and what was not. The object of this audit is the transition-probability kernel (3).
It is a real nonnegative row-stochastic matrix, exactly as the heat kernel is, and applying it to
features damps and mixes them as a diffusion step does; what it inherits from the walk is the shape
of its rows, an oscillatory profile whose width grows linearly with t rather than as a square root
(Figure 1). Once t is a trained parameter, the heat kernel reaches as far by learning a larger t,
which the right panel of Figure 5 and Table 2 show it does, in the first layer and in proportion to
the shell. What the paired tests find has to be stated in two halves. Where the design has power,
on the 264-satellite shell, the heat kernel is better on both tasks on both graphs (Tables 12 and 19);
7 of the 10 per-cell intervals that exclude zero are on that shell, and they point the same way.
On the larger shells of the torus the tests certify nothing, and the reason is stated in MAE units:
the minimum detectable effect there is 0.014 to 0.087, and the observed differences are smaller.
That is absence of evidence, not evidence of absence, and the first draft’s sentence that the kernels
“are indistinguishable” overstated a null in the same way that the preliminary version overstated a
difference. On the seam-cut graph the walk kernel takes the 1584-satellite hop field at the corrected
level. The argument of CTQW-GNN [12] for the unitary propagator in feature space, which damps
no frequency and preserves feature norm, is a different object and is neither confirmed nor refuted
here.

What the floor means for learned propagation on constellations. A budget that suffices on a 264-
satellite shell does not on a 1584-satellite one, and the failure is silent: the run reports a finite,
decreasing loss and an error that looks like a number. Carrying the constant baseline beside every
result is necessary and not sufficient: at 200 epochs the gains over it on the large shells were positive
in every cell (Table 5), and what exposed the floor was a threshold on the gain, whose value this
study set with the 4400-satellite cells in view (Section 4.2). The operative safeguard is a gain
threshold fixed and justified before the runs, and a budget that is shown to clear it on every shell;
the present study can only report that its own threshold was post hoc and that its conclusions hold
at four values of it.

                                                      26
What the seed-level bimodality and the vote noise mean. The split in Figure 4, with escape rates
of 57% and 58% (p = 0.95), means that at the preliminary budget a majority verdict counts which
seeds happened to escape. At the larger budget the majority statistic still moves by up to 3 seeds
between two correlated metrics on the same runs (Section 5.4), which is the margin by which most
of its verdicts were declared. The preliminary version’s 3-seed result at 1584 satellites sits inside
the range that this bimodality produces on the torus, where ten seeds split 5:5; on the seam-cut
graph the same cell splits 1:8 in the preliminary direction. The bimodality itself is not a property
of the seeds: Section 5.7 shows that the precision of the arithmetic moves a seed between the two
modes, so the split is a property of the training dynamics at this scale, and the number of seeds
only measures it.

Consequences for reporting. These are not new rules, and they are not the contribution; they are
what this audit needed to avoid repeating its own preliminary version, and two of them are what
its own first draft needed and lacked. Carry the constant predictor beside every result, on every
metric. Let the budget grow with the graph, or show that it need not. Treat any learned reach
parameter’s parameterisation and initialisation as an arm. Measure the reach of a “reach-matched”
baseline. Use a paired test with a multiplicity correction, state the false-positive rate of any vote
rule reported beside it, and state the minimum effect the corrected test can detect, since a null
from a test that could not reject is not a finding. Count collapsed runs and report whether they
had escaped. Check that the graph the code builds is the graph the text describes, by a property
the text can be wrong about, such as the degree sequence.

What would change the verdict. A propagator applied in feature space, unitary rather than stochas-
tic, would test the argument of CTQW-GNN [12] directly; the wave-type models PDE-GCN [24]
and graph-coupled oscillator networks [25] are the classical form of the same argument and were
not run. A learning-rate schedule would reduce the collapse rate of Section 5.7, and with it the
chaotic sensitivity to precision, and might decide cells that are now undecided; more seeds would
lower the minimum detectable effect of the pooled tests, which at 1584 satellites on the hop field
is 0.087 with ten. Other link patterns than the torus and the seam cut, and the time-varying links
of a real constellation, would say how far the seam-cut result of Section 5.5 generalises. A task
with load, in which the field to be predicted depends on traffic and not only on geometry, and a
time-varying evaluation that exercises the link churn the introduction invokes, would say whether
any operator ranking survives contact with a network function. None of these is a small addition,
and the present paper claims nothing about them.

7. Threats to validity

    The two largest shells are synthetic, and all graphs are static. The 3168- and 4400-
satellite graphs scale the Starlink shell-1 geometry and are not deployed constellations; they serve
to grow the diameter. Every instance is an independent snapshot, so the time-varying property
that motivates learned propagation on these graphs is never exercised.
    The tasks are node fields, not network functions. Hop and delay fields isolate propagation
from load, which is what the comparison needs, at the cost of distance from any routing or traffic-
engineering objective. The findings bear on the evaluation of propagation operators, not on the
routing or resource-management systems that might use them.


                                                 27
    The floor threshold was chosen with the data in view. Rule 3’s value was set after the
4400-satellite cells were seen, as Section 4.2 states; Table 20 reports every dependent count at four
values of it.
    The majority rule has a 34.4% null false-positive rate, and the per-cell test has
almost no power. The majority rule is kept only so that the preliminary verdicts are reproducible,
and no conclusion rests on it. The per-cell Holm-corrected Wilcoxon test that the first draft stood
on cannot reject in 18 of 24 cells at any effect size (Table 3); it is reported as description. The
pooled test the paper now stands on has ten seeds per test and a minimum detectable effect that
is stated beside every result; on the large shells it is 0.014 to 0.087 in MAE units, which is coarse.
    The baseline ladder is a ladder, not a tuning. α and K ′ take four values each, chosen
to bracket the measured reach of the kernels, and the best rung is selected on the same seeds the
comparison is scored on, which favours the baselines. A finer search might find a better fixed reach;
it might also find a worse one, since the ladder’s top rung already over-smooths the small shell.
    Exclusion is differential. Rule 2 removes runs after they have collapsed to the constant
solution, so it removes non-escapers preferentially, and the GCN loses more runs than the spectral
kernels (Table 21). Means are therefore optimistic, most for the GCN.
    One learning rate, one optimiser, six evaluation instances per seed. The collapse rate
is specific to Adam at 0.01 for 1000 epochs. Each seed evaluates on its own six instances, so the
sixty instances of a cell are distinct, but instance-level dispersion within a seed is not reported.
    The arithmetic is single precision, and the trajectories are chaotic. Every arm shares
one single-precision eigendecomposition per instance. Section 5.7 shows that rerunning a cell in
double precision moves individual seeds between the escaped and the collapsed mode and changes
the sign of the cell’s mean paired difference. The comparison between kernels within a precision is
fair, since both see the same rounding; but any single run’s outcome at 1584 satellites and above
is not reproducible across precisions, and a result that rests on a handful of seeds at that scale,
including the preliminary version’s, is a sample from this chaos.
    Several references are preprints. CTQW-GNN [12], CTQWformer [13] and the LEO graph-
learning work of Gu et al. [4] are unrefereed at the time of writing, and GraphQWalk [14] is a recent
journal article. The argument this audit declines to test rests on the first of these; the audit itself
rests on the preliminary version, which is not in the refereed record either.
    The preliminary artifact does not reproduce its own headline, and both versions
misdescribed their graph. The released results of the preliminary version record 3301 parameters
for the 1584-satellite experiment, which corresponds to the width-32, four-layer network its text
describes, while the released code defaults to width 16 and three layers (612 parameters) and
contains no script for that experiment. Both the preliminary version and the first drafts of this
paper state that the counter-rotating seam is cut. In the generator the flag seam=True keeps the
seam links, and every run of the two main grids was made with it; a docstring written for this study
read the flag the other way, and the text copied the docstring. The error was found by reading
Table 13, where training and test error coincide, and confirmed by the degree sequence: every node
of every main-grid snapshot has degree four. The main grids are therefore on the torus, described
as such throughout this version, and the seam-cut graph is run as its own arm (Section 5.5). The
preliminary version’s 1584-satellite claim was, in the event, made on the torus and recovered here
on the seam-cut graph; neither its text nor its authors knew which graph it was on.




                                                  28
8. Conclusion

    We audited a preliminary result of our own: that the transition-probability kernel of a continuous-
time quantum walk outperformed the heat kernel on a 1584-satellite ISL graph and did so increas-
ingly with scale. Under the preliminary budget the large shells are a floor on which no operator
learns; at the one shell where learning occurs the ranking follows the parameterisation and initialisa-
tion of the learned propagation time through a seed-level bimodality with indistinguishable escape
rates. With a budget that clears the floor, the picture depends on the graph and on the question
the test can answer: on the torus, which is the graph every main-grid run was in fact made on, the
heat kernel is better on the smallest shell on both tasks and no direction is certified on the larger
ones, where the minimum detectable effect of a ten-seed paired test is larger than any difference
observed; on the seam-cut graph, which both versions described, the preliminary direction at 1584
satellites on the hop field is recovered at the corrected level, and nothing else moves. The two
kernels are the same kind of object, a real row-stochastic propagator with a learned reach; what
the learned reach buys against a fixed reach tuned over a ladder is 16.4% at 1584 satellites in the
best t arm and a loss in the worst. What the audit adds beyond the correction of its own report
is measured: a silent budget floor that scales with the graph, a propagation-time parameterisation
that decides rankings at small budgets, a vote statistic whose noise amplitude is two to three seeds,
a per-cell corrected test that could not have rejected in three quarters of its cells, a “reach-matched”
baseline that reached half as far as the kernels it was matched to, a misread flag that put every
run on a different graph from the one described, and a training dynamics chaotic enough that
the arithmetic’s precision decides which seeds collapse. Each is a reason to distrust a small-seed
operator comparison on constellation graphs, including the one we published first and the one we
drafted second.
