         Training Budget and Time Parameterisation Decide the Ranking of
             Propagation Operators on LEO Inter-Satellite-Link Graphs

                                                Phuc Hao Doa,∗
                         a
                             CAIRA, Danang Architecture University, Da Nang, Viet Nam




Abstract
Learned propagation on the inter-satellite-link graph of a low-Earth-orbit constellation is increas-
ingly proposed for routing, state estimation and resource management, and several recent works
argue that operators derived from continuous-time quantum walks reach farther than diffusion
and should therefore help most on large graphs. We test that argument under controlled condi-
tions. Three propagation operators that share one Laplacian eigenbasis and one network, a local
GCN step, the heat kernel e−tL and the quantum-walk probability kernel |e−itL |2 , are compared on
Walker-delta shells of 264 to 4400 satellites, on two node-field tasks, over ten seeds, and across arms
that vary the parameterisation and initialisation of the learned propagation time t, the network
capacity and the training budget, for 1920 plus 360 training runs on one machine. Three findings
follow. First, with a training budget held fixed across scales, no operator improves on a constant
predictor by more than 2.8% on any shell of 1584 satellites or more, in any arm: the comparison
there measures a budget floor, not an operator. Second, on the one scale where learning occurs
under that budget, the ranking of the two spectral operators changes with how t is parameterised
and initialised, and traces to a seed-level bimodality in which a run either leaves the constant so-
lution or does not. Third, with a five-fold budget every shell leaves the floor, and wherever the
comparison is decidable the heat kernel matches or beats the walk kernel, with seed variance several
times smaller; the walk kernel wins robustly in none of 6 task-scale cells. Long training at a fixed
learning rate also collapses 11.1% of runs back to the constant solution. A preliminary version
of this study reported a walk-kernel advantage at 1584 satellites from 3 seeds; that result is not
reproduced, and its released artifact used a different configuration from its text. All code, results
and the declared protocol are released.
Keywords: LEO constellations, inter-satellite links, graph neural networks, spectral propagation,
quantum walks, evaluation methodology, reproducibility



1. Introduction

    The inter-satellite-link (ISL) graph of a low-Earth-orbit (LEO) mega-constellation is large,
nearly regular, and changes with time as satellites move and links across the seam and the po-
lar regions come and go [1, 2]. Learned models that propagate information over this graph have

work functions. Most of them are graph neural networks, and a graph neural network is, at each
layer, a propagation operator Φ(L) applied to node features followed by a shared nonlinearity [6, 7].
Which operator to use is therefore a design decision with consequences at constellation scale.
    Three operators span the design space that this paper studies. The local step of a graph
convolutional network [6], equation (1) below, moves information  √       one hop per layer. The heat
kernel e−tL moves it diffusively, over a range that grows like t [8]. The continuous-time quantum
walk [9] evolves e−itL unitarily and spreads ballistically, over a range that grows like t; its transition-
probability kernel |e−itL |2 is a real, row-stochastic matrix that can be dropped into the same
layer in place of the heat kernel [10, 11]. Recent work has turned the ballistic property into an
argument: because the unitary propagator damps no frequency and preserves feature norm, walk-
based operators should resist over-smoothing and help most where geodesics are long [12, 13, 14].
LEO ISL graphs, whose diameter grows with the number of satellites, are a natural place to look
for that benefit.
    A preliminary version of this study, circulated for review in June 2026 and available in the
companion repository, looked and reported finding it: on a 1584-satellite shell the walk kernel
reached a mean absolute error of 0.148 against 0.168 for the heat kernel, from 3 seeds, and the
trend from smaller shells was read as the ballistic mechanism emerging with scale. This paper asks
whether that ranking is a property of the operators or of the evaluation, and answers by controlling
the evaluation.
    The reason to suspect the evaluation is that three quantities were left implicit in the preliminary
comparison and in most comparisons of this kind. The first is the training budget: if the number of
epochs is held fixed while the graph grows, a model may simply not have left the constant solution
on the large graph, and a comparison between two operators that have both failed to learn measures
noise. The second is the learned propagation time t: both spectral operators carry a per-layer t
that is trained by gradient descent, and how it is parameterised and where it starts determines
how far each operator can reach and whether it can reach at all. The third is the number of seeds,
which decides whether a difference is distinguishable from run-to-run variation. We make all three
explicit and vary them.
    The contribution is a controlled comparison and what it shows.

   • A protocol in which the three operators share one eigendecomposition, one network, one pa-
     rameter count and one training loop, and in which the training budget, the t parameterisation
     and initialisation, the network capacity and the seed count are arms of the study rather than
     fixed choices. Every rule for reading the results, including the constant baseline, the floor
     below which a cell is not rankable, the seed-majority needed to call a winner, and the require-
     ment that a ranking hold in every arm, was declared before the runs and is recorded in the
     repository history.

   • Under a fixed budget of 200 epochs, no operator improves on the constant predictor by
     more than 2.8% on any shell of 1584 satellites or more, in any of eight configuration-by-arm
     combinations (Section 5.1). On the one shell where learning occurs, the heat-versus-walk
     ranking changes with the t arm, and the reason is visible at the seed level: a run either leaves
     the constant solution or stays on it (Section 5.2).

   • With a budget of 1000 epochs every shell leaves the floor. Wherever the comparison is
     decidable under the declared rules, the heat kernel matches or beats the walk kernel, with

2. Related work

Propagation operators and their reach. Spectral graph convolutions began as polynomial filters of
the Laplacian [15, 7]; the graph convolutional network of Kipf and Welling [6] keeps the first-
order term and applies a normalised adjacency once per layer, so depth bounds reach. Stacking
layers drives features toward a constant, the over-smoothing effect identified by Li et al. [16],
shown by Oono and Suzuki [17] to be exponential in depth, and surveyed by Rusch et al. [18];
remedies include decoupling propagation from transformation as in SGC [19] and personalised-
PageRank propagation [20], and residual and identity terms as in GCNII [21]. Alon and Yahav [22]
describe the complementary bottleneck by which information from exponentially many distant
nodes is squashed into fixed-size vectors, which Topping et al. [23] relate to negative curvature
of the graph. Continuous-time views replace the discrete step with a differential equation on the
graph: GRAND [8] uses diffusion, whose solution is the heat kernel; PDE-GCN [24] and graph-
coupled oscillator networks [25] use wave-type dynamics, whose reach grows linearly in time rather
than as a square root. The distinction between diffusive and ballistic spreading that motivates the
walk kernel in this paper is the same distinction, and the wave-type models are its classical form.

Quantum walks as graph operators. The continuous-time quantum walk of Farhi and Gutmann [9]
evolves an amplitude vector by e−itL and spreads ballistically; Kempe [26] and Mülken and Blu-
men [27] review the walk and its transport properties on complex networks, and Childs [28] shows
it is computationally universal. Dernbach et al. [10] and Verdon et al. [11] were among the first to
place walk-derived operators inside trainable graph models. GraphQWalk [14] builds unsupervised,
position-independent structural embeddings from walk transition probabilities. CTQWformer [13]
learns the walk Hamiltonian and feeds final-time probabilities into a graph transformer. CTQW-
GNN [12] uses the unitary propagator itself in feature space and proves that, because its eigenvalues
lie on the unit circle, no frequency is damped and the Dirichlet energy of the features cannot decay
exponentially with depth. That result concerns the unitary operator acting on features. The oper-
ator studied here is the transition-probability kernel |e−itL |2 , a real nonnegative matrix that shares
the ballistic profile of the walk but not the norm-preserving property; the distinction matters for
interpreting the results (Section 6). Quantum walks on communication networks have also been
used directly, for instance the Hadamard-walk model for network identification of Liang et al. [29].

Graph learning on LEO constellations. Handley [30] first argued that inter-satellite routing in a
mega-constellation is a latency question, Bhattacherjee and Singla [1] fix the +Grid inter-satellite
topology that most later work assumes, Kassing et al. [31] provide the Hypatia simulator for such
networks, and del Portillo et al. [2] compare the announced constellations, including the Starlink
shell whose geometry the 1584-satellite graph here reproduces. Learned models on this graph include
the link-identified routing architecture of Zhang et al. [3], the duality-guided graph learning for
joint connectivity and routing of Gu et al. [4], and machine-learning-driven cellular-satellite multi-
connectivity [5]. None of these compares propagation operators under a common parameterisation
of reach, which is the gap this study addresses. The tasks used here are deliberately decoupled from
any particular routing or traffic-engineering system, so that the propagation question is isolated
from questions of load.

Evaluation pitfalls. Shchur et al. [32] showed that the ranking of graph neural network architectures
on standard benchmarks changes with the data split and the seed, Henderson et al. [33] made
the corresponding point for deep reinforcement learning, where few seeds and unreported tuning
produce rankings that do not replicate, Bouthillier et al. [34] quantify how much benchmark variance
comes from sources other than the method, and Pineau et al. [35] report on the reproducibility
programme that turned such findings into checklists. The present study applies the same discipline
to a comparison of propagation operators, and adds two quantities specific to spectral operators,
the training budget relative to graph size and the parameterisation of the learned propagation time.

3. Setting

3.1. Constellation graphs
    Each graph is a snapshot of a Walker-delta shell. Satellite positions follow circular-orbit prop-
agation in an Earth-centred inertial frame; inter-satellite links follow the +Grid pattern [1], two
intra-plane and two inter-plane links per satellite, with inter-plane links suppressed above 70◦
latitude and across the counter-rotating seam. The graph is restricted to its largest connected com-
ponent. Four shells are used (Table 1). The 264-satellite shell is the main scale of the preliminary
version; the 1584-satellite shell reproduces the geometry of the first Starlink shell [2], 72 planes of
22 satellites at 53◦ and 550 km; the 3168- and 4400-satellite shells are scale-ups of that geometry,
72 planes of 44 and 100 planes of 44, and are synthetic in the sense that no operator flies them.
They exist to make the diameter grow.
    Each training or evaluation instance draws a snapshot time uniformly over the orbital period
and a destination satellite uniformly over the component. Instance construction is seeded, so every
instance is reproducible from its index.

where the square in (3) is taken entrywise on the modulus, so that [Φwalk ]ij is the probability that
a continuous-time quantum walk started at j is found at i after time t. Both (2) and (3) are real,

    Figure 1 makes the difference concrete on a path graph, where distance from the source is a
single coordinate. A row of the heat kernel is a bell whose spatial variance grows as t1.00 ; a row of
the walk kernel is a pair of fronts whose variance grows as t2.00 , with oscillations between them. The
exponents are fitted from the kernels themselves. This is the ballistic-versus-diffusive distinction in
its cleanest form: at equal t the walk reaches farther, and its reach grows faster with t. Whether
that translates into a better propagation operator once t is learned is the empirical question of this
paper.

4. Protocol
4.1. Parameter matching and the arms
    The three operators share depth, width, weight shapes and training loop, so a difference in
error is attributable to the operator and not to capacity. Two capacities are used: width 16 with
three layers, which is the default of the released code of the preliminary version (612 parameters
including the tℓ ), and width 32 with four layers, which the text of the preliminary version describes
and its released results were produced with (3301 parameters). Adam [36] at learning rate 0.01,
eight training and six evaluation instances per seed, and full-batch updates are used throughout.
    Four quantities are arms rather than fixed choices.
   • Parameterisation of t. A pilot run under the released parameterisation showed the learned
     tℓ going negative in the second and third layers, where relu(tℓ ) = 0 turns the propagator into
     the identity and stops its gradient, so that a model reported as three or four layers of spectral
     propagation was propagating in one. The softplus parameterisation removes this; the relu
     parameterisation is kept as a control so that the preliminary configuration is one of the arms.
   • Initialisation of t. t0 ∈ {0.5, 2}, since the same pilot showed the learned reach, and with it
     the operator ranking, depending on where t starts.
   • Capacity. The two configurations above.
   • Budget. 200 epochs, the preliminary value, held fixed across scales; and 1000 epochs for the
     second grid.
Grid 1 runs both capacities, all four t arms, four shells, two tasks, three operators and ten seeds
at 200 epochs (1920 runs). Grid 2 runs the width-32 configuration at 1000 epochs on the three
smaller shells with the two t0 = 0.5 arms (360 runs). All runs were executed on one machine, one
GPU per arm, with the eigendecomposition of each instance cached and shared so that every arm
sees bit-identical eigenpairs.

4.2. Reading rules, declared before the runs
    Five rules govern how a cell, one (task, shell, arm) combination, is read. They were fixed before
the first grid was launched, and two of them were tightened after the first runs exposed a gap; both
changes are dated in the repository and stated here.
  1. Constant baseline. Every cell reports the MAE of the predictor that outputs the mean of
     the target. Whether an operator beats it is a result, never a filter.
  2. Pathology. A run is excluded only for a training pathology: a non-finite loss, or a loss
     that rises by more than 1% between successive thirds of training. Excluded runs are counted
     next to every number they affect. A run whose loss is flat from the start because it sits on
     the constant solution is a plateau, a legitimate outcome, and is kept. The first draft of this
     rule required a 10% loss drop and would have discarded plateaus as failures; the draft was
     corrected when the first grid produced them.
  3. Floor. Let ēop be the mean MAE of an operator over its valid seeds in a cell and ē0 that of
     the constant predictor. The cell’s gain is

5.5. Cost
    Table 10 reports the median wall-clock time per run on one consumer GPU, with the eigen-
decomposition of each instance cached. The spectral operators build an N × N propagator per
layer per instance per epoch from the eigenbasis, an O(N 3 ) product that dominates at scale; the
walk kernel builds two such products, for the real and imaginary parts, and squares them. On the
4400-satellite shell at 200 epochs a walk run takes 169 s against 81 s for the heat kernel and 21 s for
the GCN, whose propagator is sparse. On CPU the same cell took of the order of an hour before
caching, which is why the preliminary version stopped at 1584 satellites. For a comparison across
scales, the cost of the operator is part of what is being compared.

here, and in the preliminary version, is the transition-probability matrix (3). It is a real nonnegative
row-stochastic matrix, exactly as the heat kernel is, and applying it to features damps and mixes

Recommendations. For anyone comparing learned propagation operators on constellation graphs,
the protocol reduces to five habits, each of which caught something in this study: report the
constant predictor beside every result; let the training budget grow with the graph, or show that
it does not need to; treat the parameterisation and initialisation of any learned reach parameter as
an arm and report the ranking in every arm; use enough seeds to apply a majority rule, and report
the majority, not only the mean; and count collapsed runs instead of dropping them. None of these
is specific to quantum-walk operators, and all of them are cheap next to the runs themselves.

What would change the verdict. Three things would. A propagator applied in feature space, uni-
tary rather than stochastic, would test the argument of CTQW-GNN [12] directly instead of the
probability kernel that inherits only its profile. A learning-rate schedule would reduce the collapse
rate of Section 5.4 and might decide the cells that are now undecided. And a task with load, in
which the field to be predicted depends on traffic and not only on geometry, would say whether the
operator ranking survives contact with a network function.

7. Threats to validity

    The two largest shells are synthetic. The 3168- and 4400-satellite graphs scale the Starlink
shell-1 geometry and are not deployed constellations. They serve to grow the diameter; conclusions
about them are conclusions about graph size, not about any operator’s network.
    The tasks are node fields, not network functions. Hop and delay fields isolate propagation
from load, which is what the comparison needs, at the cost of distance from any routing or traffic-
engineering objective. A learned model on a real objective would sit on top of these operators, and
the floor and variance effects would carry over; the ranking might not.
    One learning rate, one optimiser. The collapse rate of Section 5.4 is specific to Adam at
0.01 for 1000 epochs. A schedule would likely reduce it and might change the undecided cells. The
protocol declares the rate rather than tunes it because tuning per operator would break parameter
matching.
    The eigenbasis is cached in single precision. Training already casts the eigenpairs to single
precision, so caching them in that precision is numerically identical to recomputing, and this was
verified on a cell before the grids ran; but every arm therefore shares one eigendecomposition per
instance, and a numerical error in it would affect all arms alike.
    The reading rules are choices. The 5% floor and the seven-of-ten majority were fixed before
the runs and would give different cell verdicts at other values. Both are reported with every table
so a reader can apply their own.
    The preliminary artifact does not reproduce its own headline. The released results of
the preliminary version record 3301 parameters for the 1584-satellite experiment, which corresponds
to the width-32, four-layer network its text describes, while the released code defaults to width 16
and three layers (612 parameters) and contains no script for that experiment. The preliminary
version also ran without the seam cut that its text states. The present study fixes the seam cut on,
runs both capacities, and releases the script for every number. We state this because the protocol
of Section 4 exists to prevent exactly this class of gap, and it was found by applying the protocol
to our own earlier work.

8. Conclusion

    We asked whether the reported advantage of a quantum-walk probability kernel over the heat
kernel on large LEO inter-satellite-link graphs is a property of the operator. Under a protocol
that matches parameters, shares eigenbases, varies the parameterisation and initialisation of the
learned propagation time, doubles the capacity, raises the budget and runs ten seeds with declared
reading rules, it is not. At the preliminary budget the large shells are a floor on which no operator
learns; at the one shell where learning occurs the ranking follows the t arm through a seed-level
bimodality; and with a budget that clears the floor, the heat kernel matches or beats the walk kernel
wherever the comparison can be decided, with smaller variance. The practical recommendations
are short: carry the constant baseline, scale the budget with the graph, treat the propagation time
as a declared arm, and count collapsed runs instead of dropping them.
